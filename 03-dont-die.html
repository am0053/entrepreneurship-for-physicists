3  Don’t Die

An investor experiences the average over many companies at once. A founder experiences one company over time. For multiplicative bets those two averages are different numbers, and a bet that is excellent for the first can be ruinous for the second.

In chapter 2 we learned why to seek out bets with open upside and to take many of them. That advice has an important condition attached: you have to still be in the game to take the next one. This chapter is all about that condition. We will see how the sizing of your bets is critical to success, and what that means for how you run your company.

Let’s play a game

Sam Bankman-Fried, hereafter referred to as SBF, has famously said that he would take positive expected value (EV) bets even when the downside is total ruin. In Tyler Cowen’s March 2022 Conversations with Tyler interview, Cowen asked: “Let’s say there’s a game … 51 percent, you double the Earth out somewhere else; 49 percent, it all disappears. Would you play that game?” SBF said yes, and further said that he’d keep playing double-or-nothing. A similar situation came up at SBF’s trial, where Caroline Ellison testified that SBF once posed a coin-flip scenario where if the coin landed on tails, the world would end. But if the coin landed on heads, ‘the world would be twice as good.’ SBF said he would take that bet too. On the face of it, this could seem perfectly rational. If your expected value from a bet is positive, then isn’t it always a good bet to take?

Let’s consider a game with a slightly less extreme coin flip. On heads, your stake grows by 50%. On tails, it shrinks by 40%. The expected change per flip is \tfrac12(+50\%) + \tfrac12(-40\%) = +5\%, so the expected value is clearly positive.

Given that it’s a positive EV as a function of your stake, I invite you to stake your entire wealth, and to do so again every round. The more you bet, the more you should win, right? Not only is the expected value positive, it compounds: after 100 rounds the average wealth across all players is 1.05^{100} \approx 131 times the starting amount. So, wanna play?

The only problem with this game is that it ruins nearly everyone who plays it.

A typical player gets about fifty heads and fifty tails. Each heads-tails pair multiplies wealth by 1.5 \times 0.6 = 0.9, so after 100 rounds the median player has 0.9^{50} \approx 0.5\% of the starting stake. Only about 14% of players are ahead at all. The average of 131 is real, but it is carried by a vanishing sliver of players whose runs of heads made them astronomically rich. Playing this game is a very fat-tailed proposition. Figure 3.1 shows this with 200 players. After 100 rounds their median is 0.005, right where theory puts it, but their average is about 29, less than a quarter of the expected 131, and 83% of that comes from a single player who ended with about 4,800 times the starting stake. Two hundred players are too few to include the even rarer, even luckier ones who hold up the theoretical average.

Two averages

There are two different ways to average the growth factor of this game.

The ensemble average is taken across many players at one moment: \tfrac12(1.5) + \tfrac12(0.6) = 1.05. This is the arithmetic mean, and it is what “expected value” computes.

The time average is taken along one player’s history. Wealth after T rounds is a product of factors, so its logarithm is a sum. For very long times, the law of large numbers applies and the average growth rate of this sum converges to its expected value. In this single-path case, that average growth rate is the expected logarithm of the factor:

g = \mathbb{E}[\ln(\text{factor})] = \tfrac12 \ln 1.5 + \tfrac12 \ln 0.6 = -0.053 \text{ per round} .

Equivalently, the typical per-round factor is the geometric mean, \sqrt{1.5 \times 0.6} \approx 0.949.

This difference in averages is convex vs. concave bets and Jensen’s inequality from chapter 2 in action. The underlying random variable X is the return on a particular round, 1.5 or 0.6. In order to make a multiplicative return into a sum, we must take the logarithm, so f(X)=\ln(X). In the individual case, we get the expected value of many returns multiplied together, so we must apply the expected value after the logarithm. In the ensemble case, all of the returns apply individually and are then averaged so we can apply the logarithm after the expected value. However, because the logarithm is a concave function, Jensen’s inequality works against us, and more variance results in a worse outcome:

\mathbb{E}[\ln(X)] < \ln(\mathbb{E}[X]) with the difference becoming larger with more variance in X. Go back to SBF’s coin flips, where he is willing to bet everything on a 51% chance of a 100% gain. The expected value of this is 51\%-49\%=2\%, which is positive, so he takes it. But the expected value of the logarithms is

51\% \times \ln(2) + 49\% \times \ln(0)=0.35 - \infty=-\infty which is a better expression of what to expect as an individual taking this bet, rather than taking this bet many times in parallel in a portfolio. So, would you take it?

A system in which the time average equals the ensemble average is called ergodic, which you may have encountered as a standard assumption in a statistical mechanics course. As we have just seen, multiplicative wealth is not ergodic: the ensemble average says +5% a round while the time average says −5%. The physicist Ole Peters has argued that much confusion in economics comes from using the ensemble average where the time average is the relevant one. For example, insurance can look like a zero-sum game, where the insurer wins and the person buying insurance loses. But viewed through the time average, it can be win-win: the buyer gives up a little expected value to remove a large multiplicative loss, which raises their long-run growth rate, while the insurer, being much larger, absorbs the risk with little effect on its own growth.

Now think about you and your investor. Your investor, who has a portfolio of thirty companies where each is a small share of the fund, experiences something closer to the ensemble average. You, as founder, hold one company, essentially compounding your results over time along a single path. The same bet (your company) is thus evaluated with different mathematics by the two people at the table. When an investor says “you should take that risk” and you feel reluctant, it is quite possible that both of you are computing the exact same risk and reward, but looking at it from different perspectives.

How much to stake: the Kelly criterion

The coin game is not bad in itself; it is bad when you stake your entire wealth every time. Suppose you stake only a fraction f of your wealth each round and keep the rest in reserve. If a win pays b per unit staked with probability p, and a loss costs a per unit staked with probability q = 1 - p, the long-run growth rate is

g(f) = p \ln(1 + b f) + q \ln(1 - a f) .

Setting the derivative to zero gives the stake that maximizes long-run growth, known as the Kelly criterion after John Kelly of Bell Labs:

f^{*} = \frac{p}{a} - \frac{q}{b} .

For the coin game f^* = 0.5/0.4 - 0.5/0.5 = 25\%.

Figure 3.2 shows three significant features that carry over to real decisions:

There is an interior optimum. Staking nothing earns nothing, staking everything loses, and the best stake lies in between. The favorable odds are a reason to bet anything at all. The variance is the reason to keep most of your wealth out of any single round.

Overbetting is far worse than underbetting. The curve is nearly flat to the left of the peak and steep to the right. Staking half the optimum amount gives up only a quarter of the growth. Staking twice the optimum reduces growth to zero. Anything beyond twice the optimum turns a favorable game into a losing one. Since in real life you pretty much never know the true odds, you’re not going to be able to calculate the optimal amount to bet with 100% certainty. Given that you will probably be wrong, it is far better to err toward a smaller stake. Professional gamblers call this “half Kelly.”

The right size to bet is roughly the edge divided by the variance. For small bets the formula reduces to f^* \approx \text{edge}/\text{variance}, where edge is that positive EV. A bet with a modest edge and a huge variance deserves a small stake, however exciting its expected value. One wonders if knowing this simple formula could have saved SBF a lot of grief.

Lab 3.1: Size the Bet. Each round deals a coin game with its own odds, gain and loss. Choose the fraction of your wealth to stake on every flip, lock it in, and see how close you came to the Kelly stake. Play a few rounds before you peek at the hints.

You stake a fraction f of your current wealth on every flip. Heads, with probability p, adds b per dollar staked; tails removes a. Your score is the share of the best possible long-run growth rate g(f) = p\ln(1 + bf) + q\ln(1 - af) that your stake achieves, where the best is reached at f^* = p/a - q/b. Some games have a negative edge; the right answer to those is not to play.

Things to try

  • Play five rounds on instinct, then turn on the hints and play five more. Does edge ÷ variance get you closer?
  • After locking in, drag the slider to half the Kelly stake and then to twice it, and compare the growth at each. Which mistake costs more?
  • Find a game with a big payout on heads but a low chance of heads. How does the Kelly stake compare with what felt right?
  • Watch the single-run chart for a game where you overbet. Sometimes the overbet path is ahead for a while. Why doesn’t that make it the better choice?

What a founder can do with this

A founder cannot put 25% of themselves into a company. Your time, your reputation, and sometimes your savings are all in. However, being all-in doesn’t have to mean being totally out of the game if you lose. In Walter Isaacson’s 2023 biography, Elon Musk, he recounts a story shared by Max Levchin, Musk’s PayPal co-founder. Levchin recalls a high-stakes Texas Hold ’Em game at a friend’s bachelor pad, where Musk joined even though he wasn’t a card player. In Levchin’s words: “Elon just proceeded to go all in on every hand and lose. Then he would buy more chips and double down. Eventually, after losing many hands, he went all in and won. Then he said, ‘Right, fine, I’m done.’” In one sense, Musk was going all in. In another, he was actually staking a very small amount of his total wealth, and could easily buy back in when he lost. For your startup journey, being all in on your time and effort doesn’t necessarily mean you won’t have more time and effort to keep betting with if your company fails.

The sizing rule also applies one level down, to the bets you and the company make day to day. You can think of your company as a sequence of bets: this product, this customer segment, this hire, this production run. Each one stakes some fraction of your available resources. The Kelly sizing logic tells you the following:

  • Never stake the whole company on one roll. A single experiment that consumes the remaining runway is a full-stake coin game, whatever its expected value. Commit in stages. A smaller test or prototype that costs a fifth of the time and money while answering most of the question is nearly always available.
  • Reserves are productive. Cash that is not staked is what lets you play the next round. Treat a request to “put the money to work faster” with the suspicion it deserves.
  • Remove small recurring chances of ruin. If some practice carries a 1% chance per month of ending the company (a single supplier with no alternative, an unprotected safety hazard, one person who alone knows how the process works), the chance of surviving three years of it is only 0.99^{36} \approx 70\%. Suddenly what you might have thought of as a 1% tail risk becomes a significant 30% risk over time. Risks that repeat are much larger than they appear at first glance.
  • Size your personal stake too. A founder who has also mortgaged the house and gone without salary for two years is at full Kelly on top of full Kelly. The same goes for working yourself to the point of exhaustion. Paying yourself enough to live on and working at a sustainable level is risk management for the company, and for yourself. Also consider lowering your personal stake with a secondary sale in an earlier round of financing. Getting a meaningful amount of money for yourself personally can actually help align you with your investors’ incentives by lowering your effective stake and thus making higher variance more appealing.

None of this is an argument for timidity. Chapter 2’s lessons still apply: take every cheap convex bet you can find. The lesson from Kelly is about choosing the right size for each of these bets.

Runway is a random walk

The standard way to calculate runway is to divide cash by monthly burn. With $1.8 million in the bank and a burn of $100,000 a month, the spreadsheet says 18 months.

Real cash flows fluctuate. Customers pay late, a tool breaks, a purchase order slips a quarter. You can account for this by modeling the cash balance as a random walk: each month it changes by a drift \mu plus a random term with standard deviation \sigma. Running out of money, meaning you hit zero, functions as an absorbing barrier: a walk that touches zero stops there. For a company that is losing money on average (\mu < 0), the time to reach zero from a starting balance C_0 has

\text{mean} = \frac{C_0}{|\mu|}, \qquad \text{standard deviation} = \frac{\sigma \sqrt{C_0}}{|\mu|^{3/2}} .

With \sigma = \$60{,}000 per month, the formula gives a standard deviation of about 2.5 months in the time to reach zero. In simulations, one company in ten is out of cash by month 15, three months before the simple calculation indicated running out of money. With \sigma = \$150{,}000, which is plausible when revenue arrives in a few large payments, the standard deviation is more than six months. In simulations, a quarter of the companies are out by month 14, and one in twenty is out by month 11. That’s a whole seven months before you might have been expecting to hit zero. If you were trying to be safe and plan a six-month buffer for fundraising, there’s a not insignificant chance you are forced to start well before you planned to.

Raising money takes time, commonly several months, but that time has a long tail of its own. Presumably you will be watching your runway and will start your raise when you think you have a comfortable amount of time before going bankrupt. However, you probably also have in mind some clear milestones that you want to hit in order to be in a good position to raise, and that takes time to achieve. The implication is that if you plan all of these timelines around the mean, you are accepting even odds of running out early. Think about your runway, time to raise, and time to hit milestones as distributions, and then act based on a reasonably pessimistic assumption. Remember that going all-in and losing can work out long-term if you can keep buying back in, but if you bet everything and hit zero, that’s it.

Profitable on average is not the same as safe

Paul Graham’s test for a young company is whether it is default alive: on its current trajectory, does it reach profitability before the money runs out? Even for a narrowly profitable company, a random walk suggests danger. If the drift is positive, the probability of ever touching zero from a starting balance C_0 is

\Pr(\text{ruin}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{2 \mu C_0}{\sigma^2}\right) .

Take a hypothetical company earning $20,000 a month on average, with monthly swings of $150,000 and a cash balance of $500,000 in the bank. The exponent is 2 \times 20 \times 500 / 150^2 = 0.89, so the probability of ruin is 41%. This company is profitable and has more than two years of average profit in reserve, and yet its survival is still close to a coin flip. The dependence on the starting balance is exponential, so tripling the reserve to $1.5 million brings the risk down to 7%, almost a sixfold reduction.

Solving for the reserve that holds the risk of ruin to a chosen level gives

C_0 = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\mu} \ln\frac{1}{\Pr(\text{ruin})} .

The reserve you need grows with the square of the volatility of your cash flow and inversely with your margin. Smoothing lumpy cash flows (deposits, milestone payments, more and smaller customers) is worth far more than simply trying to increase profitability. Conversely, variability in cash flow has a real cost in terms of the reserves you need as a cushion.

Survival first, then expected value

We can summarize the takeaways from this chapter as:

  1. Along a single path, the quantity to maximize is the long-run growth rate, which is the expected value of the logarithm. This quantity penalizes variance, and it treats ruin as infinitely bad.
  2. Positive expected value is necessary for a good bet. It is not sufficient. The size of the stake relative to your reserves decides whether a good bet is good for you. Keep this in mind, and stay out of prison, and you’re already doing better than SBF.
  3. Runway is a distribution. Plan to its pessimistic tail, and remember that the reserve needed for safety scales with variance squared. Reducing variability has concrete value.
TipKey idea

Take every favorable, capped bet you can find, and size each one so that a run of losses still leaves you in the game.
