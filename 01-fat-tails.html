1  A World of Fat Tails

Most people will be familiar with the world of Gaussian or normal statistics. Take some samples, calculate a mean and standard deviation, and call it a day. Starting a company does not happen in that world. It happens in the world of fat-tailed distributions. In this chapter we arrive at three significant lessons about fat-tailed distributions:

  1. Spending time and resources to get more precise predictions of outcomes is rarely worth it.
  2. The past is a poor predictor of the possible magnitude of future outcomes.
  3. Only a few big outcomes matter, and the rest is noise.

I have written roughly forty grant proposals in the past six years, as the CTO at Irradiant. Most were for Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) grants, the federal program that funds early technical work at small companies, each asking for a few hundred thousand dollars. Every proposal takes weeks of trying to cram all of the technical detail into the page limit, making all of the budget justification numbers line up across different documents, tracking down letters of support, and always the final push wrestling with the government submission portal. All of this for some small chance of maybe getting the opportunity to get paid to do the work I really wanted to be doing.

In the moment, I always end up asking myself: is it worth it? There is a huge range in the amount of money that grants can provide. Typical Phase I SBIR grants bring in a few hundred thousand dollars, while a Phase II is often one or two million. Winning even a half dozen of these is months of effort for a few million dollars, which is not bad. But that total amount from multiple grants can then be dwarfed by a single DARPA program bringing in over $10M, even though the effort of writing that one large proposal is far less than writing six smaller proposals. Even larger R&D grant opportunities are rarer, but can reach tens to hundreds of millions. For me, the single DARPA program made up the large majority of every award I was a part of winning. That one definitely felt worth the late nights. Does that mean all of the others were a waste of time?

Is there a way of understanding, maybe even trying to predict, these kinds of opportunities? Given limited time and resources, how do we, as founders, decide where to focus our efforts, or make decisions around what is worth pursuing? If we make a series of bets, whether that’s grant proposals, or sales pitches, or VC meetings, what kind of payoff can we expect? How do we think about our odds of surviving, or even, maybe, thriving? To start even thinking about these kinds of questions, we need to have some understanding of probability and statistics beyond the everyday.

The tallest man ever measured stood 2.72 m, about 1.6 times the height of an average adult. If you met a thousand strangers and added up their heights, the tallest of them would contribute a little more than a thousandth of the total. If you had to estimate the average human height, no one you see on the street will make you think that you are radically far from the truth. This is the world of Gaussian statistics.

Now try the same exercise with companies. The largest firms in an economy have millions of times the revenue of the median firm. If you picked a thousand startups at random, a handful would account for most of the combined value and the rest would be rounding error. Meeting one more company can change your estimate of the average, by a lot. This is the world of fat-tailed statistics, a world brought to popular attention by Nassim Taleb in his book The Black Swan. In Figure 1.1 you can see that the number of US companies with at least a certain number of employees is well-fit by a power law over six orders of magnitude. If human heights followed the same distribution, the tallest person would be millions of meters tall, about ten times taller than the Earth’s atmosphere.

These are two fundamentally different worlds, and the second is the one we live in as founders. In this chapter we will explore why each of these kinds of statistics show up so frequently, and under what conditions. Then we will set out some of the consequences that the rest of this book explores.

Additive randomness and the Gaussian

Take a quantity that is the sum of many small independent contributions, none of which dominates:

X = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \cdots + \varepsilon_n .

The central limit theorem says that whatever the distribution of the individual terms (provided each has finite variance), the sum approaches a Gaussian. That is, each contribution \varepsilon_i could be a uniform distribution like a die roll, or itself a Gaussian distribution, or something lopsided like the exponential distribution of waiting times between radioactive decays, and when you add many of them together, you get a Gaussian distribution out. Thermal noise in a resistor, the position of a diffusing particle, and the error of a carefully repeated measurement are all sums of this kind. So is adult height, which depends on many genes and environmental effects of small, roughly additive size.

The feature of the Gaussian that we will focus on is the speed at which its tails die. This tells you how likely it is to see a really extreme outcome. Mathematically, the probability of landing more than z standard deviations above the mean falls like e^{-z^2/2}:

\Pr(Z > z) \approx \frac{\varphi(z)}{z} = \frac{1}{z\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2} \quad \text{for large } z .

A 3σ event (meaning an event three standard deviations from the mean) happens about once in 740 draws, a 5σ event once in 3.5 million, and a 10σ event has probability 10^{-23}. For practical purposes the distribution has a hard edge a few standard deviations out. This has some implications. One, mentioned at the beginning, is that the sample mean is stable. As you draw more and more samples, or take more and more measurements, you’re not going to get a result that fundamentally shifts the mean. This is also why outliers in an experiment can reasonably be thrown away as instrument faults, since you can safely assume that a wildly divergent measurement is some sort of fluke, and not meaningful data.

Multiplicative randomness and the log-normal

Now take a quantity that is a product of many uncertain factors:

X = f_1 \times f_2 \times \cdots \times f_n .

These kinds of quantities occur frequently. In a company, next year’s revenue is a product of this kind: the number of prospects reached, times the fraction who respond, times the fraction who buy, times the price, times how long they stay. The time to finish a task is another: the base estimate, times a factor for the thing you forgot, times a factor for the supplier who was late. Growth is a product by definition, since each year multiplies the last.

Take the logarithm and the product becomes a sum:

\ln X = \ln f_1 + \ln f_2 + \cdots + \ln f_n ,

so the central limit theorem applies to \ln X. The logarithm is thus Gaussian, and X itself is log-normal. If \ln X has mean \mu and standard deviation \sigma, then

\text{median} = e^{\mu}, \qquad \text{mean} = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad \text{mode} = e^{\mu - \sigma^2} .

The three measures of “typical” (mean, median, and mode), which are the same in a Gaussian distribution, become separated in the log-normal distribution. The specific degree of separation is determined by \sigma alone. The mode is below the median by a factor e^{\sigma^2/2}, while the mean is above it by the same factor. Here \sigma is dimensionless: it measures uncertainty in multiplicative factors. A \sigma of 0.1 means “known to about ±10%,” and the distribution is nearly Gaussian. A \sigma of 1 means “typically off by a factor of 2.7 either way,” and the distribution is strongly skewed. At \sigma = 2, the log-normal distribution is even more skewed. At this level, five outcomes out of six are below average, and one draw in a hundred holds more than a third of everything. As \sigma increases, more and more weight of the overall distribution is held in the “tail”, meaning very unlikely events have very large impact. This is the origin of the term “fat-tailed.”

Power laws

A third family has tails that are heavier still. A power-law (or Pareto) distribution has

\Pr(X > x) = \left(\frac{x_{\min}}{x}\right)^{\alpha} \quad \text{for } x \ge x_{\min} .

The exponent \alpha is called the tail index. Smaller \alpha means a fatter tail. The distribution has no characteristic scale: the chance of exceeding 2x, given that you have exceeded x, is 2^{-\alpha} no matter how large x already is. A company that has reached $10 million in revenue has the same odds of reaching $20 million as a company at $100 million has of reaching $200 million.

Power laws arise from a few generic mechanisms. One is rich get richer: when the growth of a thing is proportional to its current size, and new things keep entering at the bottom, the stationary distribution of sizes is a power law. City populations, citation counts, and firm sizes all behave this way. Another is multiplicative growth with a floor, where the floor keeps the distribution from drifting to zero and a power-law tail develops above it. City sizes are a well-studied case: each city grows or shrinks by a random percentage each year, but a town that shrinks far enough stops being counted as a city, and the economist Xavier Gabaix showed that this alone produces the power law seen in city populations. Firms are similar, since a company’s headcount changes by percentages but cannot fall below one person; it closes instead. A third mechanism to generate a power law is a mixture: log-normals with a spread of different \sigma values produce a tail that is hard to tell from a power law. Startup outcomes pooled across industries are a plausible example. Within one sector, outcomes may be roughly log-normal, but software, biotech and restaurants have very different spreads, and the pooled distribution has a heavier tail than any single sector’s.

The moments of a power law are where intuition fails. The mean is finite only when \alpha > 1, and then equals \alpha\, x_{\min}/(\alpha - 1). The variance is finite only when \alpha > 2. Many of the quantities this book cares about have a tail index between 1 and 2: a finite mean and an infinite variance. For such a quantity the sample standard deviation does not converge to anything. It simply grows with the size of the largest observation you happen to have seen.

The famous 80/20 rule, that 80% of completion is achieved with 20% of the time or effort, is a statement about \alpha. In a Pareto distribution the top fraction p of draws holds a share p^{\,1 - 1/\alpha} of the total. Setting the top 20% to hold 80% gives \alpha = \ln 5 / \ln 4 \approx 1.16. With that same exponent, the top 1% hold 53%.

I first learned about the 80/20 rule in college at Olin. Olin uses a project-based approach to engineering education, so that instead of weekly problem sets, most classes would involve several longer-term projects. Problem sets, which are curated in advance, tend to take a relatively consistent amount of time, and require a roughly consistent amount of effort for each problem. That is, they are more Gaussian in nature. Projects, in contrast, have fatter tails, and so the 80/20 rule applies. The problem, of course, is that it’s often not known in advance which 80% of a project will take 20% of the effort, versus which 20% will take 80%. This applies directly to you as a founder, since starting a company looks a lot more like a project (or a series of projects) than like a curated problem set, with the corresponding importance of log-normal or Pareto distributions. The implications of this will be explored in chapter 10.

Lab 1.1: Guess the Distribution. Each round draws numbers from a hidden distribution: a Gaussian, a log-normal, or a power law with \alpha < 2. Look at the raw values and the histogram, then name the family. Start with 20 points, then see how many you need before you can tell reliably.

Things to try

  • Play five rounds at 20 points without the summary statistics. How often can you tell a log-normal from a power law?
  • For a sample you got wrong, raise the number of points to 1,000. Which feature of the data gives the answer away?
  • Switch on logarithmic bins. A log-normal turns into a symmetric bell on that scale, while a power law keeps a long right-hand tail.
  • Turn on the summary statistics and watch “largest value ÷ median” as you add points. For a Gaussian it barely moves; for a power law it keeps growing.

Rare events

Figure 1.2 illustrates the key practical difference between the Gaussian world and the world of fat-tails. It plots the probability of exceeding a value x, where x is expressed in multiples of the median of the distribution. Consider the probabilities of seeing a few extreme values: the chance of an outcome more than ten times the median is 1% for this log-normal and 3.5% for this power law. For the Gaussian it is 10^{-72}, a number so small it has no physical meaning and is essentially zero. Said another way, for a value that you will never in the age of the universe observe from a Gaussian distribution, you will see from fat-tailed distributions in more than one in a hundred observations.

Going even more extreme, at a hundred times the median, the log-normal gives two in a million and the power law still gives one in 400. Remember that all three distributions have the same median, so in the middle of the distribution, where most of your data will be, they are hard to tell apart. And yet, in the tail, they differ by astronomical amounts. That is what it means to be fat-tailed.

Plotting data in this way can be useful to distinguish between distributions. If you plot the fraction of observations exceeding x against x on log-log paper and you see a cliff, that means thin tails. A straight line or a gentle curve that keeps going means fat tails. Distinguishing a log-normal from a power law takes a great deal of data and is rarely worth the effort, because the decisions they imply are the same. Below we outline some of the consequences of this fat-tailed behavior, which will come up again and again in this book, and in your journey as a founder.

Consequence 1: calculating the average from data doesn’t work

Everyone knows that you want more data to be able to calculate more reliable statistics. When doing an experiment, you make sure to repeat your experiment a reasonable number of times, calculate the average result, and use that. This makes a lot of sense in a Gaussian world, where the mean of n observations converges on the true mean with an error that shrinks as n^{-1/2}. Four times the data halves the error, and generally more data monotonically leads to a better estimate of the average. If you get one wildly off measurement that skews the whole average, you discard that as an outlier.

This intuition falls apart in the fat-tailed world. For a power law with 1 < \alpha < 2 the sample mean still converges, but its error shrinks only as n^{-(1 - 1/\alpha)}. At \alpha = 1.5 that is n^{-1/3}, so halving the error takes eight times the data. At \alpha = 1.16, where the 80/20 rule applies, the exponent is 0.14, and halving the error takes about 150 times the data. In the startup world, data you might want to get includes customer interest, product-market fit, whether a business model is profitable, how long it typically takes to create a new product, or whether a potential hire will be productive. These are generally not areas where it is possible to get anywhere close to 100 data points.

The situation is even worse for \alpha<1. In this case, the average is undefined. As you take more and more data, the average diverges more and more.

Figure 1.3 shows what that looks like. The Gaussian runs wobble for a few dozen observations and then settle into the true mean. The power-law runs behave differently in kind. For long stretches the running average drifts below the true mean, because the rare large values that hold much of the mass have not shown up yet. Then one arrives and the average jumps. After a thousand observations the three runs still disagree by tens of percent. The right-hand panel shows the \alpha < 1 case, where there is no true value to converge on at all.

Two important lessons follow directly from these behaviors. First, in a fat-tailed world your experience is biased toward optimism about risk, and pessimism about reward. Because most samples do not contain the rare events that dominate the mean, using past data to try to predict the future will lead you to underestimate the potential for large future changes, good or bad. A founder who has closed twenty small deals (or won twenty small grants) has learned very little about what the average deal will be once a large one lands.

Second, an error bar computed from a small fat-tailed sample should be read as a lower bound on your ignorance. In practice, when dealing with a fat-tailed distribution, as you take more samples you should expect your observed variation to grow. This is a result of hitting those rarer-but-larger events.

This does not make past data or averages useless. Means still add: the expected total of ten deals is ten times the expected deal, whatever the distribution. That fact drives chapter 10, where the mean and the median of a task’s duration turn out to be different numbers and a schedule built from medians is late almost every time. For a fat-tailed quantity, the mean is still a very useful piece of information to have, but getting an accurate estimate of the average becomes much, much more difficult than what you expect if you are used to the Gaussian world.

Consequence 2: the past is a poor bound on the future

For a Gaussian, the largest of n observations grows like \sqrt{2 \ln n} standard deviations, which is so slow that it hardly matters. A thousand observations reach about 3.2σ and a million reach about 4.9σ. The largest value you have seen is a decent guide to the largest you will see.

For a power law the largest of n observations grows like n^{1/\alpha}. With \alpha = 1.5, ten times as much experience brings a maximum 4.6 times as large. The record is always waiting to be broken by a wide margin, and the worst delay, the biggest customer and the most expensive mistake in your history say little about the next ones. If you develop a plan for the future based on “the worst case we have seen,” then that plan has been tested against a number that fat-tailed statistics promise will be exceeded. Always keep in mind the possible “Black Swan” events.

Consequence 3: only a few big things matter

In a thin-tailed world the total is made of many comparable contributions. In a fat-tailed world it is made of a few large ones plus a crowd that hardly registers (Figure 1.4). In simulations of a thousand draws, the single largest value is typically 0.16% of the total for the Gaussian, about 5% for a power law with \alpha = 1.5, and about 11% for the 80/20 power law. In about one run in twenty it is more than half.

This is possibly the most practical lesson in this book. If the value of your customers is fat-tailed, then most of your revenue will come from a few of them, and your most important job is to find and keep those few. If the value of your tasks is fat-tailed, then most of what you accomplish in a year will come from a handful of days. If investor returns are fat-tailed, then an investor’s whole fund depends on one or two companies, which explains a good deal about how investors behave, something we will explore more in chapter 2.

This characteristic of fat-tailed distributions also changes what counts as an error. In a thin-tailed world, being 10% wrong on everything costs you 10%. In a fat-tailed world, you can be wrong about almost everything and it will not matter, provided you are right about the few items that carry the total. Literally, don’t sweat the small stuff. Precision spread evenly is wasted effort. Chapter 6 develops this into a rule for where to spend measurement effort.

Which world are you in?

You rarely have enough data to fit a distribution, so often it can be better to judge by mechanism. Ask yourself these four questions:

  1. Is the quantity a sum or a product? Sums of comparable terms are thin-tailed. Products, growth processes and anything with compounding are fat-tailed.
  2. Is there a physical bound? A person cannot work 500 hours in a week or weigh a tonne. Bounded quantities are thin-tailed. Revenue, valuations, downloads and delays have no ceiling of that kind.
  3. Could one observation outweigh all the others combined? If the answer is plainly no, relax. If it is “that happens all the time,” you are in the fat-tailed world.
  4. Does success feed itself? Network effects, reputation, word of mouth and reinvested profit are all rich-get-richer mechanisms and all produce power laws.
Usually thin-tailed Usually fat-tailed
Monthly payroll and rent Outcome of a company, a product or a funding round
Unit manufacturing cost in a stable process Revenue per customer, deal size
Time for a task you have done fifty times Time for a task you have never done
Measurement error on a calibrated instrument Cost of a defect that reaches a customer
Number of hours you work in a week Value produced by one of those hours
Attendance at the weekly meeting Impact of a single hire, idea or introduction

The left column is where spreadsheets, averages and error bars behave nicely. The right column is where most of the important things in a startup live, and where we will focus our attention for the rest of this book.

TipKey idea

Before reasoning about any uncertain quantity, ask whether its randomness multiplies, or ask if there are “rich-get-richer” feedback mechanisms. If so, put yourself in the fat-tailed mindset: think in factors and logarithms, describe it with quantiles, and expect the total to be dominated by a few draws.

Lab 1.2: Tail Explorer. Draw samples from a Gaussian, a log-normal and a power law with adjustable tail weight. Watch the running average, the largest draw and the share held by the top 1% as the sample grows, then resample and see how much the answers move.

Draw a sample from one of three families and watch the statistics you would normally trust. The Gaussian has mean 1. The log-normal has median 1 and a spread \sigma in its logarithm. The power law has \Pr(X > x) = x^{-\alpha} for x \ge 1. The tail-weight slider moves \sigma from 0.2 to 2.5, or \alpha from 3 down to 1.05.

Things to try

  • Select the power law at a tail weight near 0.95 (\alpha \approx 1.15, the 80/20 case). Draw new samples several times and watch “sample mean ÷ true mean”. How often is it below 1?
  • Keep the same setting and raise the number of draws from 100 to 100,000. Does the largest draw’s share of the total shrink the way it would for a Gaussian?
  • Switch to the Gaussian. Nothing you do with the sliders will make the top 1% hold more than a few percent.
  • Find the log-normal \sigma at which the top 1% of draws hold a quarter of the total.

Further reading

  • M. E. J. Newman, Power laws, Pareto distributions and Zipf’s law (2005). The best single review for a physicist, with the generating mechanisms worked out.
  • A. Clauset, C. R. Shalizi and M. E. J. Newman, Power-law distributions in empirical data (2009). How to test for a power law properly, and how often published claims fail the test.
  • N. N. Taleb, Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails (2020). Technical and opinionated. The first few chapters cover why standard estimators fail.
  • M. Mitzenmacher, A brief history of generative models for power law and lognormal distributions, Internet Mathematics (2004). Shows how small changes in a multiplicative process move you between the two families.
  • X. Gabaix, Zipf’s law for cities: an explanation, Quarterly Journal of Economics (1999). Random percentage growth with a lower bound produces a power law.
  • US Census Bureau, Statistics of U.S. Businesses. Annual counts of firms by employment size, the source for the firm-size figure.
  • R. L. Axtell, Zipf distribution of U.S. firm sizes, Science (2001). The evidence that firm sizes follow a power law with an exponent close to 1.
